Feb 2025A consumer app with wallet payments, built from zero

SplytWeChat mini programmeRole PM for the mini programme

Problem

Splyt sold rides through partners' apps. The mini programme was its first app of its own, for Chinese travellers booking rides abroad inside WeChat. The hard part was money: the final fare isn't known until the trip ends, the rider pays in a different currency, and an unpaid balance from a traveller is hard to recover.

What I did

  • Defined the full user journey with a contracted designer, and set funnel tracking to find where users dropped off.
  • Designed the payment flow with our payment partner: charge upfront on the estimate plus a buffer, then refund or top up automatically once the final fare is known.
  • Set fare buffers per market from sampled trip data, added an FX buffer, and blocked new bookings while a balance was unpaid.
  • Fixed drop-off points: replaced a "no service available" first screen with destination suggestions, and added "I'm booking for someone else" with a country selector.
  • Led QA through launch and go-to-market with external agencies; set up settlement reconciliation with Finance.

The live app

Screens recorded from the live mini programme. Personal details, addresses and maps are blurred.

Key decision

Charge upfront with a buffer, not after the trip. The rider sees a slightly higher charge, and the difference is refunded automatically. In return, the fare was secured before the trip, and defaults stayed low.

Outcome

Launched in February 2025 with automatic refunds and top-ups. The unpaid-balance block kept defaults low, and the conversion fixes came straight from funnel data.

2026Fixing a rule that stranded riders

Splyt × Kakao MobilitySouth KoreaRole PM for the Kakao partnership

Problem

Sometimes a driver never picked the rider up, but the booking stayed "on board", meaning the system believed the passenger was already in the car. A rider can't cancel a trip that's on board, and Splyt's API allows only one active booking per splyt_user_ID, so they couldn't book another ride either. They were stuck until support got the supplier to cancel it, which could take an hour or more.

To keep riders from being blocked, Kakao created a new splyt_user_ID for every booking, so one Kakao user could have many IDs. Splyt could no longer tell it was the same person: the one-booking rule and the freeze after three cancellations in an hour stopped working, and it clashed with Grab's need for one ID per user. Our monitors started flagging riders with ten or more cancellations in a row.

What I did

  • Asked why, instead of enforcing the rule. Kakao's real concern was stuck bookings: riders, sometimes families late at night, left waiting an hour or more with no way to cancel and book again.
  • Designed a middle path, agreed with our head of engineering and CTO, then aligned Kakao on it: one ID per real user, so the abuse rule still works, plus a secondary ID used only to rebook a genuinely stuck booking.

Key decision

Keep the rule, remove the reason to bypass it. I first offered to raise the cancellation limit, but that didn't solve Kakao's real worry. A secondary ID only for stuck bookings kept the protection, met Grab's need for one ID per user, and got stranded riders moving again.

Outcome

Kakao reported 30% faster resolution of stuck bookings. Riders with a stuck booking could book a new ride within minutes instead of waiting up to an hour for support.

Dec 2025 – Sep 2026Moving a live platform onto a new supplier API

Splyt × GrabRole PM for the Grab partnership

Problem

Grab launched a new external API that needed user details before it would return a price. Splyt's API didn't collect them, and every demand partner built on top of it. A clumsy change would have forced every partner to rework their integration.

What I did

  • Built a proof of concept that met Grab's requirement without changing partners' flows, at the cost of a very rare price mismatch. Grab didn't accept it, so after talks between Splyt's CEO, CTO and Grab, we agreed to collect user details upfront.
  • Redesigned the flow to collect user details upfront, and released it inside an existing endpoint so partners' work stayed small.
  • Built a new address-search endpoint for Grab's places requirement, and briefed every partner with a clear plan and dates.
  • Moved traffic partner by partner, starting with our own mini programme, so problems surfaced where we controlled the fix.

Key decision

Meet the supplier's requirement, but keep the cost to partners small. Once leadership agreed to Grab's terms, I added the new fields to an endpoint partners already used, and sent the plan weeks before release, so most partners had little to rebuild.

Outcome

Our integration was complete, and every partner had implemented the user-details change, before I handed over in September 2026.

Q3 2024Saying no to protect fair pricing

Splyt × HeycarsRole PM for the Heycars partnership

Problem

Heycars wanted riders to request several car types at once, which is standard in China, and had heard a verbal go-ahead in a meeting. Sending many requests at the same time would inflate demand, trigger surge pricing and get drivers cancelled on, putting our supplier relationships at risk.

What I did

  • With our head of engineering, told Heycars clearly on the call: "Please don't build this. Let me clarify with our head of product and come back to you."
  • Used booking data showing drivers were assigned within 3 to 5 minutes, so the feature wasn't needed for fast pickups.
  • Held firm when Heycars suggested sending the requests one after another instead.
  • Closed the loop with our head of product straight after the call, and came back with a way forward: work with Grab on a proper solution. Helped Heycars explain realistic wait times to its riders.

Key decision

One clear no, with a way forward. Faster pickups for one partner weren't worth unfair prices across the whole marketplace. After hearing yes and then no, the partner needed one unmistakable message.

Outcome

Heycars paused the work and explained wait times to its riders, and the partnership kept growing. My lesson: partners should hear one voice, so feature commitments should go through a quick product and engineering check first.

Dec 2025 – Mar 2026Testing before giving up a cheaper car type

Splyt × GrabWeChat mini programmeRole PM for the Grab partnership

Problem

Grab wanted to stop offering Grab Saver, its cheapest car type, through Splyt. Most of our demand came from price-sensitive Chinese travellers, with Chinese New Year coming up.

What I did

  • Pushed back, and with the Splyt team moved the removal to the end of March 2026, after the Chinese New Year peak.
  • With our senior data analyst, ran an A/B test on the mini programme from mid-February to the end of March, with 200+ users per group, showing estimates with and without Saver.
  • When Grab also asked us to sort premium car types first, flagged that two changes at once could muddy the results, and kept the Saver test clean by giving both groups the same sorting.

Key decision

Settle the debate with a clean test. Instead of arguing opinions with a bigger partner, we measured what riders chose, and protected the test from a second change running at the same time.

Outcome

Riders preferred Saver: keeping it gave a small but statistically significant lift in conversion. Premium-first sorting showed no significant change, so we kept it. Splyt held on to Saver through its busiest season.

Protecting the marketplace from being bypassed

SplytContracts, product and monitoringRole PM for the platform

Problem

Splyt sits between apps that sell rides and the suppliers that provide them, so partners on either side can try to cut it out, and some people try to game the system.

What I did

  • During reselling talks in Shanghai, Heycars quietly tried to contact Grab directly, and Grab told us. I helped write new clauses on data safety and storage, markups and exclusivity, and, as Heycars' PM, judged how much API detail to share.
  • Suppliers used our rider-driver chat to push riders to WhatsApp or their own apps. We got them to stop under the contract, and I specified a filter for links, phone numbers, emails and keywords. It was deprioritised and not built.
  • Some drivers in Indonesia booked rides for themselves through unverified accounts to collect waiting fees. We reported the drivers to Grab and the accounts to the demand partner.
  • Watched live bookings and alerts, and investigated anything unusual in Kibana.

Key decision

Use contracts, product and monitoring together. A clause only works if someone notices it being broken, and a product fix takes time to build. Using all three covered the gaps in each.

Outcome

New clauses were in place for Heycars, suppliers stopped sending redirect messages, and the accounts behind the waiting-fee loophole were reported.

2023 – 2026Fixing tax, currency and payment problems with a supplier

Splyt × BoltEurope and Middle EastRole PM for the Bolt partnership

Problem

Bolt was Splyt's only supplier in Europe and the Middle East. Bolt UAE's finance team verified our payments late, so our account went negative and service stopped, a top-priority incident. Bolt's prices didn't say whether a driver had to charge VAT, so we charged VAT on every ride. And one UK account billed all non-UAE rides in GBP without the local currency or exchange rate, which made them hard to reconcile.

What I did

  • Escalated the UAE suspensions to Bolt's partner manager and pushed for a higher credit limit, so a late check wouldn't stop service.
  • Got Bolt to show VAT on the receipt in 2026.
  • Specified what Bolt's monthly transaction list needed: local currency, GBP amount and the rate used.
  • When figures didn't match, checked our backend logs against the partner's records to find the right amount, and mediated disputes.

Key decision

Fix the data at the source. Reconciling around missing data every month costs Finance time forever. Getting the supplier to send the right data fixes it once.

Outcome

VAT appears on receipts, a higher credit limit gave a buffer against late checks, and Bolt agreed the new currency reporting, which was in development when I left.

Sep 2026 – nowAI app development: directing agents and checking their output

Personal projectExercise LogBuilt with Claude Code agent teams177 commits

Problem

I've logged my gym sessions in a phone notes app for about ten years, and beginners have no easy way to look up an exercise they don't know. I wanted a workout tracker with an exercise library, where AI fills gaps without making things up. Someone following a wrong exercise can get hurt, and AI output sounds just as confident when it's wrong.

What I did

  • Agentic workflow: wrote the PRD, data model and flows, then orchestrated a team of Claude Code agents to build a full-stack app (React, Node, Supabase), reviewing and steering their work.
  • Free first, AI on request: searches hit the saved library first (including 776 exercises imported from Wger), then Wikipedia. Claude only runs when the user clicks Generate.
  • RAG and grounding: Claude writes the exercise from the retrieved Wikipedia article, passed in as delimited sources, and is told to ground the guide in them.
  • Tool calling and structured output: the model must call a save tool whose schema enforces 3 to 10 steps, specific muscle names and clean equipment tags.
  • Guardrails and hallucination prevention: automatic rule checks, a block on medical-claim language such as "cures" or "prevents injury", a check that every muscle named is actually in the source text, and escaping of source text so it can't break out of its tags.
  • Human in the loop: generated exercises are held as pending, visible only to the person who asked, with a disclaimer. A reviewer approves or rejects them with a reason before anyone else sees them.
  • Token economics: a daily AI quota, a 7-day query cache, reuse of existing exercises instead of a new call, source text trimmed to the relevant 3,000 characters, and token and latency logging on every call.
  • Evals: wrote the evaluation spec and a 50-exercise gold set; scoring is the next step.

The app

Key decision

Free first, AI on request, a human before anyone else sees it. Most searches never call the model, which keeps costs bounded. When it does run, it writes from a real source, and the checks plus a reviewer decide what is good enough to share.

Sep 2024A self-serve sandbox guide that replaced partner kick-off calls

SplytPartner onboardingSandbox guide + user flowsRole PM, my own initiative

Problem

Every new partner started with a technical kick-off call. First we had to find a time that worked for the partner's team, a Splyt engineer, BD and me (product), often across time zones. On the call, we demonstrated the API with a simulator in the sandbox. The real questions only came after the call, once partner engineers started building, which meant more emails, more calls and more waiting. Many partners had never built ride-hailing before, so they also didn't know what each response should look like to a rider.

What I did

  • Wrote a sandbox guide that includes access tokens and a link to the driver simulator tool, and walks through every endpoint in the API: what to send, what comes back, and how to handle it.
  • Set out the services partners needed to bring themselves, such as map tiles, address search and routing.
  • Paired each endpoint with a screen from a reference user flow I drew, so engineers could see how the data should appear in their app, from quotes to receipt.
  • Covered the edge cases partners tended to miss: predefined pickup points, prohibited areas, accepting supplier terms before payment, and driver chat.
  • Made it reusable for every new partner.

Key decision

Replace the call with something partners can keep. A call needs everyone in the same hour and is forgotten by the time engineers start building. A guide answers questions when they come up, at the partner's own pace.

Outcome

No more scheduling kick-off calls. Partner kick-off time fell by about a week across 10+ partners, and the guide is still used to onboard partners today.

2020 – 2021Running a connected training system for seniors

PulseSyncConnected hardwareRole Programme executive

Problem

Gym Tonic was a strength-training programme for seniors on hydraulic machines. Each user's card loaded their programme onto the machine's tablet, and each centre tracked clients' loads on a dashboard. It needed hardware, software and trainers to work together.

What I did

  • Gave Tier 1 support across hardware, tablet and software, and trained trainers at partner organisations.
  • Improved the Gym Tonic training guide: redrew the machine's tablet screens in Illustrator, filmed step-by-step how-to videos with a trainer, and rewrote the instructions with clearer graphics.

Key decision

Learn every layer of the system. Fixing the machines myself kept centres running, and showed me how hardware, software and people fit together.

Outcome

Machines stayed in service while the manufacturer's engineers couldn't travel, and trainer and client feedback shaped the product.
